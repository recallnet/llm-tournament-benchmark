The **Internet** (or **internet**)<sup id="cite_ref-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-1"><span>[</span>a<span>]</span></a></sup> is the [global system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_network "Global network") of interconnected [computer networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_network "Computer network") that uses the [Internet protocol suite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_protocol_suite "Internet protocol suite") (TCP/IP)<sup id="cite_ref-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-3"><span>[</span>b<span>]</span></a></sup> to communicate between networks and devices. It is a [network of networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internetworking "Internetworking") that consists of [private](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_network "Private network"), public, academic, business, and government networks of local to global scope, linked by a broad array of electronic, [wireless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_network "Wireless network"), and [optical networking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_networking "Optical networking") technologies. The Internet carries a vast range of information resources and services, such as the interlinked [hypertext](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypertext "Hypertext") documents and [applications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_application "Web application") of the [World Wide Web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Wide_Web "World Wide Web") (WWW), [electronic mail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Email "Email"), [internet telephony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_telephony "Internet telephony"), [streaming media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media "Streaming media") and [file sharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File_sharing "File sharing").

The origins of the Internet date back to research that enabled the [time-sharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-sharing "Time-sharing") of computer resources, the development of [packet switching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Packet_switching "Packet switching") in the 1960s and the design of computer networks for [data communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_communication "Data communication").<sup id="cite_ref-The_Washington_Post_4-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-The_Washington_Post-4"><span>[</span>2<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-:6_5-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-:6-5"><span>[</span>3<span>]</span></a></sup> The set of rules ([communication protocols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_protocol "Communication protocol")) to enable [internetworking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internetworking "Internetworking") on the Internet arose from research and development commissioned in the 1970s by the [Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defense_Advanced_Research_Projects_Agency "Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency") (DARPA) of the [United States Department of Defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Defense "United States Department of Defense") in collaboration with universities and researchers across the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States") and in the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom "United Kingdom") and [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France "France").<sup id="cite_ref-Abbatep3_6-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-Abbatep3-6"><span>[</span>4<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-7"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-7"><span>[</span>5<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-8"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-8"><span>[</span>6<span>]</span></a></sup> The [ARPANET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ARPANET "ARPANET") initially served as a backbone for the interconnection of regional academic and military networks in the United States to enable [resource sharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resource_sharing "Resource sharing"). The funding of the [National Science Foundation Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Science_Foundation_Network "National Science Foundation Network") as a new backbone in the 1980s, as well as private funding for other commercial extensions, encouraged worldwide participation in the development of new networking technologies and the merger of many networks using DARPA's [Internet protocol suite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_protocol_suite "Internet protocol suite").<sup id="cite_ref-9"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-9"><span>[</span>7<span>]</span></a></sup> The linking of commercial networks and enterprises by the early 1990s, as well as the advent of the [World Wide Web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Wide_Web "World Wide Web"),<sup id="cite_ref-10"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-10"><span>[</span>8<span>]</span></a></sup> marked the beginning of the transition to the modern Internet,<sup id="cite_ref-11"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-11"><span>[</span>9<span>]</span></a></sup> and generated sustained exponential growth as generations of institutional, [personal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_computer "Personal computer"), and [mobile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_device "Mobile device") [computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer "Computer") were connected to the internetwork. Although the Internet was widely used by [academia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academia "Academia") in the 1980s, the subsequent [commercialization of the Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercialization_of_the_Internet "Commercialization of the Internet") in the 1990s and beyond incorporated its services and technologies into virtually every aspect of modern life.

Most traditional communication media, including [telephone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone "Telephone"), [radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio "Radio"), [television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television "Television"), paper mail, and newspapers, are reshaped, redefined, or even bypassed by the Internet, giving birth to new services such as [email](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Email "Email"), [Internet telephone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_telephone "Internet telephone"), [Internet radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_radio "Internet radio"), [Internet television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_television "Internet television"), [online music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_music "Online music"), digital newspapers, and [audio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio_stream "Audio stream") and [video streaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_streaming "Video streaming") websites. Newspapers, books, and other print publishing have adapted to [website](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_site "Web site") technology or have been reshaped into [blogging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blogging "Blogging"), [web feeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_feed "Web feed"), and online [news aggregators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_aggregator "News aggregator"). The Internet has enabled and accelerated new forms of personal interaction through [instant messaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant_messaging "Instant messaging"), [Internet forums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_forum "Internet forum"), and [social networking services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_networking_service "Social networking service"). [Online shopping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_shopping "Online shopping") has grown exponentially for major retailers, [small businesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_business "Small business"), and [entrepreneurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entrepreneur "Entrepreneur"), as it enables firms to extend their "[brick and mortar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brick_and_mortar "Brick and mortar")" presence to serve a larger market or even [sell goods and services entirely online](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_store "Online store"). [Business-to-business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business-to-business "Business-to-business") and [financial services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_services "Financial services") on the Internet affect [supply chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supply_chain "Supply chain") across entire industries.

The Internet has no single centralized governance in either technological implementation or policies for access and usage; each constituent network sets its own policies.<sup id="cite_ref-12"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-12"><span>[</span>10<span>]</span></a></sup> The overarching definitions of the two principal [name spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Name_space "Name space") on the Internet, the [Internet Protocol address](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_address "IP address") (IP address) space and the [Domain Name System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_Name_System "Domain Name System") (DNS), are directed by a maintainer organization, the [Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Corporation_for_Assigned_Names_and_Numbers "Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers") (ICANN). The technical underpinning and standardization of the core protocols is an activity of the [Internet Engineering Task Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Engineering_Task_Force "Internet Engineering Task Force") (IETF), a non-profit organization of loosely affiliated international participants that anyone may associate with by contributing technical expertise.<sup id="cite_ref-13"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-13"><span>[</span>11<span>]</span></a></sup> In November 2006, the Internet was included on _[USA Today](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USA_Today "USA Today")_'s list of the [New Seven Wonders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wonders_of_the_World#USA_Today's_New_Seven_Wonders "Wonders of the World").<sup id="cite_ref-14"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-14"><span>[</span>12<span>]</span></a></sup>

## Terminology

The word _internetted_ was used as early as 1849, meaning _interconnected_ or _interwoven_.<sup id="cite_ref-15"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-15"><span>[</span>13<span>]</span></a></sup> The word _Internet_ was used in 1945 by the United States War Department in a radio operator's manual,<sup id="cite_ref-16"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-16"><span>[</span>14<span>]</span></a></sup> and in 1974 as the shorthand form of Internetwork.<sup id="cite_ref-RFC675_17-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-RFC675-17"><span>[</span>15<span>]</span></a></sup> Today, the term _Internet_ most commonly refers to the global system of interconnected [computer networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_network "Computer network"), though it may also refer to any group of smaller networks.<sup id="cite_ref-The_New_York_Times_18-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-The_New_York_Times-18"><span>[</span>16<span>]</span></a></sup>

In common use most publications treated the word _Internet_ as a capitalized [proper noun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_noun_and_common_noun "Proper noun and common noun"); this has become less common.<sup id="cite_ref-The_New_York_Times_18-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-The_New_York_Times-18"><span>[</span>16<span>]</span></a></sup> This reflects the tendency in English to capitalize new terms and move them to lowercase as they become familiar.<sup id="cite_ref-The_New_York_Times_18-2"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-The_New_York_Times-18"><span>[</span>16<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Wired_19-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-Wired-19"><span>[</span>17<span>]</span></a></sup> The word is sometimes still capitalized to distinguish the global internet from smaller networks, though many publications, including the _[AP Stylebook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AP_Stylebook "AP Stylebook")_ since 2016, recommend the lowercase form in every case.<sup id="cite_ref-The_New_York_Times_18-3"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-The_New_York_Times-18"><span>[</span>16<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Wired_19-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-Wired-19"><span>[</span>17<span>]</span></a></sup> In 2016, the _[Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary "Oxford English Dictionary")_ found that, based on a study of around 2.5 billion printed and online sources, "Internet" was capitalized in 54% of cases.<sup id="cite_ref-20"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-20"><span>[</span>18<span>]</span></a></sup>

The terms _Internet_ and _[World Wide Web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Wide_Web "World Wide Web")_ are often used interchangeably; it is common to speak of "going on the Internet" when using a [web browser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_browser "Web browser") to view [web pages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_page "Web page"). However, the [World Wide Web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Wide_Web "World Wide Web"), or _the Web_, is only one of a large number of Internet services,<sup id="cite_ref-21"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-21"><span>[</span>19<span>]</span></a></sup> a collection of documents (web pages) and other [web resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_resource "Web resource") linked by [hyperlinks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperlink "Hyperlink") and [URLs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_resource_locator "Uniform resource locator").<sup id="cite_ref-22"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-22"><span>[</span>20<span>]</span></a></sup>

## History

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d6/A_sketch_of_the_ARPANET_in_December_1969.png/250px-A_sketch_of_the_ARPANET_in_December_1969.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:A_sketch_of_the_ARPANET_in_December_1969.png)

A sketch of the [ARPANET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ARPANET "ARPANET") in December 1969. The nodes at [UCLA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Los_Angeles "University of California, Los Angeles") and the [Stanford Research Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SRI_International "SRI International") (SRI) are among those depicted.<sup id="cite_ref-23"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-23"><span>[</span>21<span>]</span></a></sup>

In the 1960s, [computer scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_scientists "Computer scientists") began developing systems for [time-sharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-sharing "Time-sharing") of computer resources.<sup id="cite_ref-Lee1992_24-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-Lee1992-24"><span>[</span>22<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-ctsspg_25-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-ctsspg-25"><span>[</span>23<span>]</span></a></sup> [J. C. R. Licklider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._C._R._Licklider "J. C. R. Licklider") proposed the idea of a universal network while working at [Bolt Beranek & Newman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolt_Beranek_%26_Newman "Bolt Beranek & Newman") and, later, leading the [Information Processing Techniques Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_Processing_Techniques_Office "Information Processing Techniques Office") (IPTO) at the [Advanced Research Projects Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Research_Projects_Agency "Advanced Research Projects Agency") (ARPA) of the United States [Department of Defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Defense "United States Department of Defense") (DoD). Research into [packet switching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Packet_switching "Packet switching"), one of the fundamental Internet technologies, started in the work of [Paul Baran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Baran "Paul Baran") at [RAND](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAND "RAND") in the early 1960s and, independently, [Donald Davies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Davies "Donald Davies") at the United Kingdom's [National Physical Laboratory](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Physical_Laboratory_(United_Kingdom)> "National Physical Laboratory (United Kingdom)") (NPL) in 1965.<sup id="cite_ref-The_Washington_Post_4-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-The_Washington_Post-4"><span>[</span>2<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-NIHF2007_26-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-NIHF2007-26"><span>[</span>24<span>]</span></a></sup> After the [Symposium on Operating Systems Principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symposium_on_Operating_Systems_Principles "Symposium on Operating Systems Principles") in 1967, packet switching from the proposed [NPL network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NPL_network "NPL network") and routing concepts proposed by Baran were incorporated into the design of the [ARPANET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ARPANET "ARPANET"), an experimental [resource sharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resource_sharing "Resource sharing") network proposed by ARPA.<sup id="cite_ref-27"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-27"><span>[</span>25<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-28"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-28"><span>[</span>26<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-29"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-29"><span>[</span>27<span>]</span></a></sup>

ARPANET development began with two network nodes which were interconnected between the [University of California, Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Los_Angeles "University of California, Los Angeles") (UCLA) and the [Stanford Research Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SRI_International "SRI International") (now SRI International) on 29 October 1969.<sup id="cite_ref-NetValley_30-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-NetValley-30"><span>[</span>28<span>]</span></a></sup> The third site was at the [University of California, Santa Barbara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Santa_Barbara "University of California, Santa Barbara"), followed by the [University of Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Utah "University of Utah"). In a sign of future growth, 15 sites were connected to the young ARPANET by the end of 1971.<sup id="cite_ref-31"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-31"><span>[</span>29<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-32"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-32"><span>[</span>30<span>]</span></a></sup> These early years were documented in the 1972 film _[Computer Networks: The Heralds of Resource Sharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_Networks:_The_Heralds_of_Resource_Sharing "Computer Networks: The Heralds of Resource Sharing")_.<sup id="cite_ref-33"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-33"><span>[</span>31<span>]</span></a></sup> Thereafter, the ARPANET gradually developed into a decentralized communications network, connecting remote centers and military bases in the United States.<sup id="cite_ref-34"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-34"><span>[</span>32<span>]</span></a></sup> Other user networks and research networks, such as the [Merit Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merit_Network "Merit Network") and [CYCLADES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CYCLADES "CYCLADES"), were developed in the late 1960s and early 1970s.<sup id="cite_ref-35"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-35"><span>[</span>33<span>]</span></a></sup>

Early international collaborations for the ARPANET were rare. Connections were made in 1973 to Norway ([NORSAR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NORSAR "NORSAR") and [NDRE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Defence_Research_Establishment "Norwegian Defence Research Establishment")),<sup id="cite_ref-36"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-36"><span>[</span>34<span>]</span></a></sup> and to [Peter Kirstein's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_T._Kirstein "Peter T. Kirstein") research group at [University College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_London "University College London") (UCL), which provided a gateway to [British academic networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_in_the_United_Kingdom#History "Internet in the United Kingdom"), forming the first [internetwork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internetworking "Internetworking") for [resource sharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resource_sharing "Resource sharing").<sup id="cite_ref-37"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-37"><span>[</span>35<span>]</span></a></sup> ARPA projects, the [International Network Working Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Network_Working_Group "International Network Working Group") and commercial initiatives led to the development of various [protocols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_protocol "Communication protocol") and standards by which multiple separate networks could become a single network or "a network of networks".<sup id="cite_ref-38"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-38"><span>[</span>36<span>]</span></a></sup> In 1974, [Vint Cerf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vint_Cerf "Vint Cerf") at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University "Stanford University") and [Bob Kahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Kahn "Bob Kahn") at DARPA published a proposal for "A Protocol for Packet Network Intercommunication".<sup id="cite_ref-IEEE_Transactions_on_Communications_39-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-IEEE_Transactions_on_Communications-39"><span>[</span>37<span>]</span></a></sup> Cerf and his students used the term _internet_ as a shorthand for _internetwork_ in _[RFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RFC_(identifier) "RFC (identifier)") [675](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc675)\_,<sup id="cite_ref-RFC675_17-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-RFC675-17"><span>[</span>15<span>]</span></a></sup> and later [RFCs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Request_for_Comments "Request for Comments") repeated this use. Cerf and Kahn credit [Louis Pouzin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Pouzin "Louis Pouzin") and others with important influences on the resulting [TCP/IP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TCP/IP "TCP/IP") design.<sup id="cite_ref-IEEE_Transactions_on_Communications_39-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-IEEE_Transactions_on_Communications-39"><span>[</span>37<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-40"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-40"><span>[</span>38<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-41"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-41"><span>[</span>39<span>]</span></a></sup> National [PTTs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postal,_telegraph_and_telephone_service "Postal, telegraph and telephone service") and commercial providers developed the [X.25](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X.25 "X.25") standard and deployed it on [public data networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_data_network "Public data network").<sup id="cite_ref-42"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-42"><span>[</span>40<span>]</span></a></sup>

Access to the ARPANET was expanded in 1981 when the [National Science Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Science_Foundation "National Science Foundation") (NSF) funded the [Computer Science Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CSNET "CSNET") (CSNET). In 1982, the [Internet Protocol Suite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Protocol_Suite "Internet Protocol Suite") (TCP/IP) was standardized, which facilitated worldwide proliferation of interconnected networks. TCP/IP network access expanded again in 1986 when the [National Science Foundation Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Science_Foundation_Network "National Science Foundation Network") (NSFNet) provided access to [supercomputer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercomputer "Supercomputer") sites in the United States for researchers, first at speeds of 56 kbit/s and later at 1.5 Mbit/s and 45 Mbit/s.<sup id="cite_ref-43"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-43"><span>[</span>41<span>]</span></a></sup> The NSFNet expanded into academic and research organizations in Europe, Australia, New Zealand and Japan in 1988–89.<sup id="cite_ref-44"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-44"><span>[</span>42<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-45"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-45"><span>[</span>43<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-46"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-46"><span>[</span>44<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-47"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-47"><span>[</span>45<span>]</span></a></sup> Although other network protocols such as [UUCP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UUCP "UUCP") and PTT public data networks had global reach well before this time, this marked the beginning of the Internet as an intercontinental network. Commercial [Internet service providers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_service_providers "Internet service providers") (ISPs) emerged in 1989 in the United States and Australia.<sup id="cite_ref-48"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-48"><span>[</span>46<span>]</span></a></sup> The ARPANET was decommissioned in 1990.<sup id="cite_ref-49"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-49"><span>[</span>47<span>]</span></a></sup>

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e5/NSFNET-backbone-T3.png/250px-NSFNET-backbone-T3.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:NSFNET-backbone-T3.png)

T3 [NSFNET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NSFNET "NSFNET") Backbone, c. 1992

Steady advances in [semiconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor "Semiconductor") technology and [optical networking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_networking "Optical networking") created new economic opportunities for commercial involvement in the expansion of the network in its core and for delivering services to the public. In mid-1989, [MCI Mail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MCI_Mail "MCI Mail") and [Compuserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compuserve "Compuserve") established connections to the Internet, delivering email and public access products to the half million users of the Internet.<sup id="cite_ref-50"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-50"><span>[</span>48<span>]</span></a></sup> Just months later, on 1 January 1990, PSInet launched an alternate Internet backbone for commercial use; one of the networks that added to the core of the commercial Internet of later years. In March 1990, the first high-speed T1 (1.5 Mbit/s) link between the NSFNET and Europe was installed between [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University "Cornell University") and [CERN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CERN "CERN"), allowing much more robust communications than were capable with satellites.<sup id="cite_ref-51"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-51"><span>[</span>49<span>]</span></a></sup>

Later in 1990, [Tim Berners-Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Berners-Lee "Tim Berners-Lee") began writing [WorldWideWeb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WorldWideWeb "WorldWideWeb"), the first [web browser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_browser "Web browser"), after two years of lobbying CERN management. By Christmas 1990, Berners-Lee had built all the tools necessary for a working Web: the [HyperText Transfer Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HyperText_Transfer_Protocol "HyperText Transfer Protocol") (HTTP) 0.9,<sup id="cite_ref-52"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-52"><span>[</span>50<span>]</span></a></sup> the [HyperText Markup Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HyperText_Markup_Language "HyperText Markup Language") (HTML), the first Web browser (which was also an [HTML editor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTML_editor "HTML editor") and could access [Usenet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usenet "Usenet") newsgroups and [FTP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FTP "FTP") files), the first HTTP [server software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Server_application "Server application") (later known as [CERN httpd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CERN_httpd "CERN httpd")), the first [web server](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_server "Web server"),<sup id="cite_ref-53"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-53"><span>[</span>51<span>]</span></a></sup> and the first Web pages that described the project itself. In 1991 the [Commercial Internet eXchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Internet_eXchange "Commercial Internet eXchange") was founded, allowing PSInet to communicate with the other commercial networks [CERFnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CERFnet "CERFnet") and Alternet. [Stanford Federal Credit Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_Federal_Credit_Union "Stanford Federal Credit Union") was the first [financial institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_institution "Financial institution") to offer online Internet banking services to all of its members in October 1994.<sup id="cite_ref-54"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-54"><span>[</span>52<span>]</span></a></sup> In 1996, [OP Financial Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OP_Financial_Group "OP Financial Group"), also a [cooperative bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_bank "Cooperative bank"), became the second online bank in the world and the first in Europe.<sup id="cite_ref-55"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-55"><span>[</span>53<span>]</span></a></sup> By 1995, the Internet was fully commercialized in the U.S. when the NSFNet was decommissioned, removing the last restrictions on use of the Internet to carry commercial traffic.<sup id="cite_ref-ConneXions-April1996_56-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-ConneXions-April1996-56"><span>[</span>54<span>]</span></a></sup>

<table><caption><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_number_of_Internet_users" title="List of countries by number of Internet users">Worldwide Internet users</a><sup id="cite_ref-ITCFactsAndFigures-2023_57-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-ITCFactsAndFigures-2023-57"><span>[</span>55<span>]</span></a></sup></caption><tbody><tr><th></th><th scope="col">2005</th><th scope="col">2010</th><th scope="col">2017</th><th scope="col">2023</th></tr><tr><th scope="row">World population (billions)<sup id="cite_ref-WWIU_USCensus-WorldPop1950to2050-2014_58-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-WWIU_USCensus-WorldPop1950to2050-2014-58"><span>[</span>56<span>]</span></a></sup></th><td>6.5</td><td>6.9</td><td>7.4</td><td>8.0</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">Worldwide</th><td>16%</td><td>30%</td><td>48%</td><td>67%</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">In developing world</th><td>8%</td><td>21%</td><td>41.3%</td><td>60%</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">In developed world</th><td>51%</td><td>67%</td><td>81%</td><td>93%</td></tr></tbody></table>

As technology advanced and commercial opportunities fueled reciprocal growth, the volume of [Internet traffic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_traffic "Internet traffic") started experiencing similar characteristics as that of the scaling of [MOS transistors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOS_transistor "MOS transistor"), exemplified by [Moore's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore%27s_law "Moore's law"), doubling every 18 months. This growth, formalized as [Edholm's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edholm%27s_law "Edholm's law"), was catalyzed by advances in [MOS technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOS_technology "MOS technology"), [laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser "Laser") light wave systems, and [noise](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise_(signal_processing)> "Noise (signal processing)") performance.<sup id="cite_ref-Jindal_59-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-Jindal-59"><span>[</span>57<span>]</span></a></sup>

Since 1995, the Internet has tremendously impacted culture and commerce, including the rise of near-instant communication by email, [instant messaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant_messaging "Instant messaging"), telephony ([Voice over Internet Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice_over_Internet_Protocol "Voice over Internet Protocol") or VoIP), [two-way interactive video calls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_chat "Video chat"), and the World Wide Web<sup id="cite_ref-60"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-60"><span>[</span>58<span>]</span></a></sup> with its [discussion forums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discussion_forums "Discussion forums"), blogs, [social networking services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_networking_service "Social networking service"), and [online shopping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_shopping "Online shopping") sites. Increasing amounts of data are transmitted at higher and higher speeds over fiber optic networks operating at 1 Gbit/s, 10 Gbit/s, or more. The Internet continues to grow, driven by ever-greater amounts of online information and knowledge, commerce, entertainment and social networking services.<sup id="cite_ref-61"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-61"><span>[</span>59<span>]</span></a></sup> During the late 1990s, it was estimated that traffic on the public Internet grew by 100 percent per year, while the mean annual growth in the number of Internet users was thought to be between 20% and 50%.<sup id="cite_ref-62"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-62"><span>[</span>60<span>]</span></a></sup> This growth is often attributed to the lack of central administration, which allows organic growth of the network, as well as the non-proprietary nature of the Internet protocols, which encourages vendor interoperability and prevents any one company from exerting too much control over the network.<sup id="cite_ref-63"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-63"><span>[</span>61<span>]</span></a></sup> As of 31 March 2011, the estimated total number of Internet users was 2.095 billion (30% of [world population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_population "World population")).<sup id="cite_ref-stats1_64-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-stats1-64"><span>[</span>62<span>]</span></a></sup> It is estimated that in 1993 the Internet carried only 1% of the information flowing through two-way [telecommunication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunication "Telecommunication"). By 2000 this figure had grown to 51%, and by 2007 more than 97% of all telecommunicated information was carried over the Internet.<sup id="cite_ref-65"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-65"><span>[</span>63<span>]</span></a></sup>

## Governance

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/46/Icannheadquartersplayavista.jpg/250px-Icannheadquartersplayavista.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Icannheadquartersplayavista.jpg)

ICANN headquarters in the [Playa Vista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playa_Vista,_Los_Angeles "Playa Vista, Los Angeles") neighborhood of [Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles "Los Angeles"), California, United States

The Internet is a [global network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_network "Global network") that comprises many voluntarily interconnected autonomous networks. It operates without a central governing body. The technical underpinning and standardization of the core protocols ([IPv4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPv4 "IPv4") and [IPv6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPv6 "IPv6")) is an activity of the [Internet Engineering Task Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Engineering_Task_Force "Internet Engineering Task Force") (IETF), a non-profit organization of loosely affiliated international participants that anyone may associate with by contributing technical expertise. To maintain interoperability, the principal [name spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Name_space "Name space") of the Internet are administered by the [Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Corporation_for_Assigned_Names_and_Numbers "Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers") (ICANN). ICANN is governed by an international board of directors drawn from across the Internet technical, business, academic, and other non-commercial communities. ICANN coordinates the assignment of unique identifiers for use on the Internet, including [domain names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_name "Domain name"), IP addresses, application port numbers in the transport protocols, and many other parameters. Globally unified name spaces are essential for maintaining the global reach of the Internet. This role of ICANN distinguishes it as perhaps the only central coordinating body for the global Internet.<sup id="cite_ref-66"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-66"><span>[</span>64<span>]</span></a></sup>

[Regional Internet registries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_Internet_registry "Regional Internet registry") (RIRs) were established for five regions of the world. The [African Network Information Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AfriNIC "AfriNIC") (AfriNIC) for [Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa "Africa"), the [American Registry for Internet Numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Registry_for_Internet_Numbers "American Registry for Internet Numbers") (ARIN) for [North America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_America "North America"), the [Asia–Pacific Network Information Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/APNIC "APNIC") (APNIC) for [Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia "Asia") and the [Pacific region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_region "Pacific region"), the [Latin American and Caribbean Internet Addresses Registry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_American_and_Caribbean_Internet_Addresses_Registry "Latin American and Caribbean Internet Addresses Registry") (LACNIC) for [Latin America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_America "Latin America") and the [Caribbean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caribbean "Caribbean") region, and the [Réseaux IP Européens – Network Coordination Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RIPE_NCC "RIPE NCC") (RIPE NCC) for [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe"), the [Middle East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East "Middle East"), and [Central Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asia "Central Asia") were delegated to assign IP address blocks and other Internet parameters to local registries, such as [Internet service providers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_service_provider "Internet service provider"), from a designated pool of addresses set aside for each region.

The [National Telecommunications and Information Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Telecommunications_and_Information_Administration "National Telecommunications and Information Administration"), an agency of the [United States Department of Commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Commerce "United States Department of Commerce"), had final approval over changes to the [DNS root zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNS_root_zone "DNS root zone") until the IANA stewardship transition on 1 October 2016.<sup id="cite_ref-67"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-67"><span>[</span>65<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-68"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-68"><span>[</span>66<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-69"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-69"><span>[</span>67<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-70"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-70"><span>[</span>68<span>]</span></a></sup> The [Internet Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Society "Internet Society") (ISOC) was founded in 1992 with a mission to _"assure the open development, evolution and use of the Internet for the benefit of all people throughout the world"_.<sup id="cite_ref-71"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-71"><span>[</span>69<span>]</span></a></sup> Its members include individuals (anyone may join) as well as corporations, [organizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organizations "Organizations"), governments, and universities. Among other activities ISOC provides an administrative home for a number of less formally organized groups that are involved in developing and managing the Internet, including: the IETF, [Internet Architecture Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Architecture_Board "Internet Architecture Board") (IAB), [Internet Engineering Steering Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Engineering_Steering_Group "Internet Engineering Steering Group") (IESG), [Internet Research Task Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Research_Task_Force "Internet Research Task Force") (IRTF), and [Internet Research Steering Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Research_Steering_Group "Internet Research Steering Group") (IRSG). On 16 November 2005, the United Nations-sponsored [World Summit on the Information Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Summit_on_the_Information_Society "World Summit on the Information Society") in [Tunis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunis "Tunis") established the [Internet Governance Forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Governance_Forum "Internet Governance Forum") (IGF) to discuss Internet-related issues.

## Infrastructure

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7d/World_map_of_submarine_cables.png/250px-World_map_of_submarine_cables.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:World_map_of_submarine_cables.png)

2007 map showing submarine fiberoptic telecommunication cables around the world

The communications infrastructure of the Internet consists of its hardware components and a system of software layers that control various aspects of the architecture. As with any computer network, the Internet physically consists of [routers](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Router_(computing)> "Router (computing)"), media (such as cabling and radio links), repeaters, modems etc. However, as an example of [internetworking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internetworking "Internetworking"), many of the network nodes are not necessarily Internet equipment per se. The internet packets are carried by other full-fledged networking protocols with the Internet acting as a homogeneous networking standard, running across [heterogeneous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterogeneous "Heterogeneous") hardware, with the packets guided to their destinations by IP routers.

[Colocation centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colocation_center "Colocation center") often host [private peering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_peering "Private peering") connections between their customers, [internet transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_transit "Internet transit") providers, cloud providers,<sup id="cite_ref-72"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-72"><span>[</span>70<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-73"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-73"><span>[</span>71<span>]</span></a></sup> [meet-me rooms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meet-me_room "Meet-me room") for connecting customers together<sup id="cite_ref-74"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-74"><span>[</span>72<span>]</span></a></sup> [Internet exchange points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_exchange_point "Internet exchange point"),<sup id="cite_ref-75"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-75"><span>[</span>73<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-76"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-76"><span>[</span>74<span>]</span></a></sup> and landing points and terminal equipment for fiber optic [submarine communication cables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_communication_cable "Submarine communication cable"),<sup id="cite_ref-77"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-77"><span>[</span>75<span>]</span></a></sup> connecting the internet.<sup id="cite_ref-78"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-78"><span>[</span>76<span>]</span></a></sup>

### Service tiers

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/36/Internet_Connectivity_Distribution_%26_Core.svg/250px-Internet_Connectivity_Distribution_%26_Core.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Internet_Connectivity_Distribution_%26_Core.svg)

Packet routing across the Internet involves several tiers of Internet service providers.

[Internet service providers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_service_provider "Internet service provider") (ISPs) establish the worldwide connectivity between individual networks at various levels of scope. End-users who only access the Internet when needed to perform a function or obtain information, represent the bottom of the routing hierarchy. At the top of the routing hierarchy are the [tier 1 networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tier_1_network "Tier 1 network"), large telecommunication companies that exchange traffic directly with each other via very high speed [fiber-optic cables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiber-optic_cable "Fiber-optic cable") and governed by [peering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peering "Peering") agreements. [Tier 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tier_2_network "Tier 2 network") and lower-level networks buy [Internet transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_transit "Internet transit") from other providers to reach at least some parties on the global Internet, though they may also engage in peering. An ISP may use a single upstream provider for connectivity, or implement [multihoming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multihoming "Multihoming") to achieve redundancy and load balancing. [Internet exchange points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_exchange_point "Internet exchange point") are major traffic exchanges with physical connections to multiple ISPs. Large organizations, such as academic institutions, large enterprises, and governments, may perform the same function as ISPs, engaging in peering and purchasing transit on behalf of their internal networks. Research networks tend to interconnect with large subnetworks such as [GEANT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GEANT "GEANT"), [GLORIAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GLORIAD "GLORIAD"), [Internet2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet2 "Internet2"), and the UK's [national research and education network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_research_and_education_network "National research and education network"), [JANET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JANET "JANET").

### Access

Common methods of [Internet access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_access "Internet access") by users include dial-up with a computer [modem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modem "Modem") via telephone circuits, [broadband](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadband "Broadband") over [coaxial cable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coaxial_cable "Coaxial cable"), [fiber optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_fiber "Optical fiber") or copper wires, [Wi-Fi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wi-Fi "Wi-Fi"), [satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_Internet "Satellite Internet"), and [cellular telephone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_telephony "Mobile telephony") technology (e.g. [3G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3G "3G"), [4G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4G "4G")). The Internet may often be accessed from computers in libraries and [Internet cafés](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_caf%C3%A9 "Internet café"). [Internet access points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_kiosk "Internet kiosk") exist in many public places such as airport halls and coffee shops. Various terms are used, such as _public Internet kiosk_, _public access terminal_, and _Web [payphone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Payphone "Payphone")_. Many hotels also have public terminals that are usually fee-based. These terminals are widely accessed for various usages, such as ticket booking, bank deposit, or [online payment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_payment "Online payment"). Wi-Fi provides wireless access to the Internet via local computer networks. [Hotspots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wi-Fi_hotspot "Wi-Fi hotspot") providing such access include Wi-Fi cafés, where users need to bring their own wireless devices, such as a laptop or [PDA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_Digital_Assistant "Personal Digital Assistant"). These services may be free to all, free to customers only, or fee-based.

[Grassroots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grassroots "Grassroots") efforts have led to [wireless community networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_community_network "Wireless community network"). Commercial [Wi-Fi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wi-Fi "Wi-Fi") services that cover large areas are available in many cities, such as [New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City "New York City"), [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London "London"), [Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna "Vienna"), [Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toronto "Toronto"), [San Francisco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco "San Francisco"), [Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia "Philadelphia"), [Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago "Chicago") and [Pittsburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh "Pittsburgh"), where the Internet can then be accessed from places such as a park bench.<sup id="cite_ref-79"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-79"><span>[</span>77<span>]</span></a></sup> Experiments have also been conducted with proprietary mobile wireless networks like [Ricochet](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricochet_(Internet_service)> "Ricochet (Internet service)"), various high-speed data services over cellular networks, and fixed wireless services. Modern [smartphones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smartphone "Smartphone") can also access the Internet through the cellular carrier network. For Web browsing, these devices provide applications such as [Google Chrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Chrome "Google Chrome"), [Safari](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safari_(web_browser)> "Safari (web browser)"), and [Firefox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firefox "Firefox") and a wide variety of other Internet software may be installed from [app stores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/App_store "App store"). Internet usage by mobile and tablet devices exceeded desktop worldwide for the first time in October 2016.<sup id="cite_ref-80"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-80"><span>[</span>78<span>]</span></a></sup>

#### Mobile communication

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e4/Number_of_mobile_cellular_subscriptions_2012-2016.svg/250px-Number_of_mobile_cellular_subscriptions_2012-2016.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Number_of_mobile_cellular_subscriptions_2012-2016.svg)

Number of mobile cellular subscriptions 2012–2016

The [International Telecommunication Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Telecommunication_Union "International Telecommunication Union") (ITU) estimated that, by the end of 2017, 48% of individual users regularly connect to the Internet, up from 34% in 2012.<sup id="cite_ref-81"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-81"><span>[</span>79<span>]</span></a></sup> [Mobile Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_Web "Mobile Web") connectivity has played an important role in expanding access in recent years, especially in [Asia and the Pacific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia%E2%80%93Pacific "Asia–Pacific") and in Africa.<sup id="cite_ref-UNESCO_82-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-UNESCO-82"><span>[</span>80<span>]</span></a></sup> The number of unique mobile cellular subscriptions increased from 3.9 billion in 2012 to 4.8 billion in 2016, two-thirds of the world's population, with more than half of subscriptions located in Asia and the Pacific. The number of subscriptions was predicted to rise to 5.7 billion users in 2020.<sup id="cite_ref-GSMA_The_Mobile_Economy_2019_83-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-GSMA_The_Mobile_Economy_2019-83"><span>[</span>81<span>]</span></a></sup> As of 2018, 80% of the world's population were covered by a [4G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4G "4G") network.<sup id="cite_ref-GSMA_The_Mobile_Economy_2019_83-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-GSMA_The_Mobile_Economy_2019-83"><span>[</span>81<span>]</span></a></sup> The limits that users face on accessing information via mobile applications coincide with a broader process of [fragmentation of the Internet](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fragmentation_(computing)> "Fragmentation (computing)"). Fragmentation restricts access to media content and tends to affect the poorest users the most.<sup id="cite_ref-UNESCO_82-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-UNESCO-82"><span>[</span>80<span>]</span></a></sup>

[Zero-rating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-rating "Zero-rating"), the practice of Internet service providers allowing users free connectivity to access specific content or applications without cost, has offered opportunities to surmount economic hurdles but has also been accused by its critics as creating a two-tiered Internet. To address the issues with zero-rating, an alternative model has emerged in the concept of 'equal rating' and is being tested in experiments by [Mozilla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozilla "Mozilla") and [Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_S.A. "Orange S.A.") in Africa. Equal rating prevents prioritization of one type of content and zero-rates all content up to a specified data cap. In a study published by [Chatham House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chatham_House "Chatham House"), 15 out of 19 countries researched in Latin America had some kind of hybrid or zero-rated product offered. ## Internet Protocol Suite

The Internet standards describe a framework known as the [Internet protocol suite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_protocol_suite "Internet protocol suite") (also called [TCP/IP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TCP/IP "TCP/IP"), based on the first two components.) This is a suite of protocols that are ordered into a set of four conceptional [layers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_protocol#Layering "Communication protocol") by the scope of their operation, originally documented in [RFC](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RFC_(identifier)> "RFC (identifier)") [1122](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1122) and [RFC](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RFC_(identifier)> "RFC (identifier)") [1123](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1123). At the top is the [application layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Application_layer "Application layer"), where communication is described in terms of the objects or data structures most appropriate for each application. For example, a web browser operates in a [client–server](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Client%E2%80%93server_model "Client–server model") application model and exchanges information with the [HyperText Transfer Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HyperText_Transfer_Protocol "HyperText Transfer Protocol") (HTTP) and an application-germane data structure, such as the [HyperText Markup Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTML "HTML") (HTML).

Below this top layer, the [transport layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_layer "Transport layer") connects applications on different hosts with a logical channel through the network. It provides this service with a variety of possible characteristics, such as ordered, reliable delivery (TCP), and an unreliable datagram service (UDP).

Underlying these layers are the networking technologies that interconnect networks at their borders and exchange traffic across them. The [Internet layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_layer "Internet layer") implements the [Internet Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Protocol "Internet Protocol") (IP) which enables computers to identify and locate each other by [IP address](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_address "IP address") and route their traffic via intermediate (transit) networks.<sup id="cite_ref-rfc791_87-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-rfc791-87"><span>[</span>85<span>]</span></a></sup> The Internet Protocol layer code is independent of the type of network that it is physically running over.

At the bottom of the architecture is the [link layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Link_layer "Link layer"), which connects nodes on the same physical link, and contains protocols that do not require routers for traversal to other links. The protocol suite does not explicitly specify hardware methods to transfer bits, or protocols to manage such hardware, but assumes that appropriate technology is available. Examples of that technology include [Wi-Fi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wi-Fi "Wi-Fi"), [Ethernet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethernet "Ethernet"), and [DSL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DSL "DSL").

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3b/UDP_encapsulation.svg/250px-UDP_encapsulation.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:UDP_encapsulation.svg)

As user data is processed through the protocol stack, each abstraction layer adds encapsulation information at the sending host. Data is transmitted _over the wire_ at the link level between hosts and routers. Encapsulation is removed by the receiving host. Intermediate relays update link encapsulation at each hop, and inspect the IP layer for routing purposes.

### Internet protocol

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c4/IP_stack_connections.svg/250px-IP_stack_connections.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:IP_stack_connections.svg)

Conceptual data flow in a simple network topology of two hosts (_A_ and _B_) connected by a link between their respective routers. The application on each host executes read and write operations as if the processes were directly connected to each other by some kind of data pipe. After the establishment of this pipe, most details of the communication are hidden from each process, as the underlying principles of communication are implemented in the lower protocol layers. In analogy, at the transport layer the communication appears as host-to-host, without knowledge of the application data structures and the connecting routers, while at the internetworking layer, individual network boundaries are traversed at each router.

The most prominent component of the Internet model is the Internet Protocol (IP). IP enables internetworking and, in essence, establishes the Internet itself. Two versions of the Internet Protocol exist, [IPv4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPv4 "IPv4") and [IPv6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPv6 "IPv6").

#### IP Addresses

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a5/Example_of_an_iterative_DNS_resolver.svg/250px-Example_of_an_iterative_DNS_resolver.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Example_of_an_iterative_DNS_resolver.svg)

A DNS resolver consults three name servers to resolve the domain name user-visible "www.wikipedia.org" to determine the IPv4 Address 207.142.131.234.

For locating individual computers on the network, the Internet provides [IP addresses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_address "IP address"). IP addresses are used by the Internet infrastructure to direct internet packets to their destinations. They consist of fixed-length numbers, which are found within the packet. IP addresses are generally assigned to equipment either automatically via [DHCP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DHCP "DHCP"), or are configured.

However, the network also supports other addressing systems. Users generally enter [domain names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fully_qualified_domain_name "Fully qualified domain name") (e.g. "en.wikipedia.org") instead of IP addresses because they are easier to remember; they are converted by the [Domain Name System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_Name_System "Domain Name System") (DNS) into IP addresses which are more efficient for routing purposes.

#### IPv4

[Internet Protocol version 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPv4 "IPv4") (IPv4) defines an IP address as a [32-bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/32-bit "32-bit") number.<sup id="cite_ref-rfc791_87-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-rfc791-87"><span>[</span>85<span>]</span></a></sup> IPv4 is the initial version used on the first generation of the Internet and is still in dominant use. It was designed in 1981 to address up to ≈4.3 billion (10<sup>9</sup>) hosts. However, the explosive growth of the Internet has led to [IPv4 address exhaustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPv4_address_exhaustion "IPv4 address exhaustion"), which entered its final stage in 2011,<sup id="cite_ref-88"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-88"><span>[</span>86<span>]</span></a></sup> when the global IPv4 address allocation pool was exhausted.

#### IPv6

Because of the growth of the Internet and the [depletion of available IPv4 addresses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPv4_address_exhaustion "IPv4 address exhaustion"), a new version of IP [IPv6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPv6 "IPv6"), was developed in the mid-1990s, which provides vastly larger addressing capabilities and more efficient routing of Internet traffic. IPv6 uses 128 bits for the IP address and was standardized in 1998.<sup id="cite_ref-rfc1883_89-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-rfc1883-89"><span>[</span>87<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-rfc2460_90-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-rfc2460-90"><span>[</span>88<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-rfc8200_91-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-rfc8200-91"><span>[</span>89<span>]</span></a></sup> [IPv6 deployment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPv6_deployment "IPv6 deployment") has been ongoing since the mid-2000s and is currently in growing deployment around the world, since Internet address registries ([RIRs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_Internet_registry "Regional Internet registry")) began to urge all resource managers to plan rapid adoption and conversion.<sup id="cite_ref-92"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-92"><span>[</span>90<span>]</span></a></sup>

IPv6 is not directly interoperable by design with IPv4. In essence, it establishes a parallel version of the Internet not directly accessible with IPv4 software. Thus, translation facilities must exist for internetworking or nodes must have duplicate networking software for both networks. Essentially all modern computer operating systems support both versions of the Internet Protocol. Network infrastructure, however, has been lagging in this development. Aside from the complex array of physical connections that make up its infrastructure, the Internet is facilitated by bi- or multi-lateral commercial contracts, e.g., [peering agreements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peering_agreement "Peering agreement"), and by technical specifications or protocols that describe the exchange of data over the network. Indeed, the Internet is defined by its interconnections and routing policies.

#### Subnetwork

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/14/Subnetting_Concept-en.svg/330px-Subnetting_Concept-en.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Subnetting_Concept-en.svg)

Creating a subnet by dividing the host identifier

A _[subnetwork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subnetwork "Subnetwork")_ or _subnet_ is a logical subdivision of an [IP network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_network "IP network").<sup id="cite_ref-rfc950_93-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-rfc950-93"><span>[</span>91<span>]</span></a></sup><sup><span title="Page / location: 1, 16">: 1, 16 </span></sup>  The practice of dividing a network into two or more networks is called _subnetting_. Computers that belong to a subnet are addressed with an identical [most-significant bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Most-significant_bit "Most-significant bit")\-group in their IP addresses. This results in the logical division of an IP address into two fields, the _network number_ or _routing prefix_ and the _rest field_ or _host identifier_. The _rest field_ is an identifier for a specific [host](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host_(network)> "Host (network)") or network interface.

The _routing prefix_ may be expressed in [Classless Inter-Domain Routing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classless_Inter-Domain_Routing "Classless Inter-Domain Routing") (CIDR) notation written as the first address of a network, followed by a slash character (_/_), and ending with the bit-length of the prefix. For example, _198.51.100.0/24_ is the prefix of the [Internet Protocol version 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Protocol_version_4 "Internet Protocol version 4") network starting at the given address, having 24 bits allocated for the network prefix, and the remaining 8 bits reserved for host addressing. Addresses in the range _198.51.100.0_ to _198.51.100.255_ belong to this network. The IPv6 address specification _2001:db8::/32_ is a large address block with 2<sup>96</sup> addresses, having a 32-bit routing prefix.

For IPv4, a network may also be characterized by its _subnet mask_ or _netmask_, which is the [bitmask](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitmask "Bitmask") that when applied by a [bitwise AND](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitwise_AND "Bitwise AND") operation to any IP address in the network, yields the routing prefix. Subnet masks are also expressed in [dot-decimal notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot-decimal_notation "Dot-decimal notation") like an address. For example, _255.255.255.0_ is the subnet mask for the prefix _198.51.100.0/24_.

Traffic is exchanged between subnetworks through routers when the routing prefixes of the source address and the destination address differ. A router serves as a logical or physical boundary between the subnets.

The benefits of subnetting an existing network vary with each deployment scenario. In the address allocation architecture of the Internet using CIDR and in large organizations, it is necessary to allocate address space efficiently. Subnetting may also enhance routing efficiency or have advantages in network management when subnetworks are administratively controlled by different entities in a larger organization. Subnets may be arranged logically in a hierarchical architecture, partitioning an organization's network address space into a tree-like routing structure.

#### Routing

Computers and routers use [routing tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Routing_table "Routing table") in their operating system to [direct IP packets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_forwarding "IP forwarding") to reach a node on a different subnetwork. Routing tables are maintained by manual configuration or automatically by [routing protocols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Routing_protocol "Routing protocol"). End-nodes typically use a [default route](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Default_route "Default route") that points toward an ISP providing transit, while ISP routers use the [Border Gateway Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border_Gateway_Protocol "Border Gateway Protocol") to establish the most efficient routing across the complex connections of the global Internet. The [default gateway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Default_gateway "Default gateway") is the [node](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Node_(networking)> "Node (networking)") that serves as the forwarding host (router) to other networks when no other route specification matches the destination [IP address](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_address "IP address") of a packet.<sup id="cite_ref-94"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-94"><span>[</span>92<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-95"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-95"><span>[</span>93<span>]</span></a></sup>

### IETF

While the hardware components in the Internet infrastructure can often be used to support other software systems, it is the design and the standardization process of the software that characterizes the Internet and provides the foundation for its scalability and success. The responsibility for the architectural design of the Internet software systems has been assumed by the [Internet Engineering Task Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Engineering_Task_Force "Internet Engineering Task Force") (IETF).<sup id="cite_ref-96"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-96"><span>[</span>94<span>]</span></a></sup> The IETF conducts standard-setting work groups, open to any individual, about the various aspects of Internet architecture. The resulting contributions and standards are published as _[Request for Comments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Request_for_Comments "Request for Comments")_ (RFC) documents on the IETF web site. The principal methods of networking that enable the Internet are contained in specially designated RFCs that constitute the [Internet Standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Standard "Internet Standard"). Other less rigorous documents are simply informative, experimental, or historical, or document the best current practices (BCP) when implementing Internet technologies.

## Applications and services

The Internet carries many [applications and services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_service "Network service"), most prominently the World Wide Web, including [social media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_media "Social media"), [electronic mail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_mail "Electronic mail"), [mobile applications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_app "Mobile app"), [multiplayer online games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplayer_online_game "Multiplayer online game"), [Internet telephony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_telephony "Internet telephony"), [file sharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File_sharing "File sharing"), and [streaming media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media "Streaming media") services. Most [servers](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Server_(computing)> "Server (computing)") that provide these services are today hosted in [data centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_center "Data center"), and content is often accessed through high-performance [content delivery networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Content_delivery_network "Content delivery network").

### World Wide Web

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d1/First_Web_Server.jpg/250px-First_Web_Server.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:First_Web_Server.jpg)

This [NeXT Computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NeXT_Computer "NeXT Computer") was used by [Tim Berners-Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Berners-Lee "Tim Berners-Lee") at [CERN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CERN "CERN") and became the world's first [Web server](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_server "Web server").

The World Wide Web is a global collection of [documents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Documents "Documents"), [images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_graphics "Computer graphics"), [multimedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multimedia "Multimedia"), applications, and other resources, logically interrelated by [hyperlinks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperlink "Hyperlink") and referenced with [Uniform Resource Identifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_Resource_Identifier "Uniform Resource Identifier") (URIs), which provide a global system of named references. URIs symbolically identify services, [web servers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_servers "Web servers"), databases, and the documents and resources that they can provide. [HyperText Transfer Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HyperText_Transfer_Protocol "HyperText Transfer Protocol") (HTTP) is the main access protocol of the World Wide Web. [Web services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_service "Web service") also use HTTP for communication between software systems for information transfer, sharing and exchanging business data and logistics and is one of many languages or protocols that can be used for communication on the Internet.<sup id="cite_ref-97"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-97"><span>[</span>95<span>]</span></a></sup>

World Wide Web browser software, such as [Microsoft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft "Microsoft")'s [Internet Explorer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Explorer "Internet Explorer")/[Edge](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Edge_(series_of_web_browsers)> "Microsoft Edge (series of web browsers)"), [Mozilla Firefox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozilla_Firefox "Mozilla Firefox"), [Opera](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opera_(web_browser)> "Opera (web browser)"), [Apple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Inc. "Apple Inc.")'s [Safari](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safari_(web_browser)> "Safari (web browser)"), and [Google Chrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Chrome "Google Chrome"), enable users to navigate from one web page to another via the hyperlinks embedded in the documents. These documents may also contain any combination of [computer data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_data "Computer data"), including graphics, sounds, [text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plain_text "Plain text"), [video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_video "Web video"), [multimedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multimedia "Multimedia") and interactive content that runs while the user is interacting with the page. [Client-side software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Client-side_scripting "Client-side scripting") can include animations, [games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_game "Web game"), [office applications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_applications "Office applications") and scientific demonstrations. Through [keyword](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keyword_(Internet_search)> "Keyword (Internet search)")\-driven [Internet research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_research "Internet research") using [search engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_search_engine "Web search engine") like [Yahoo!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahoo!_Search "Yahoo! Search"), [Bing](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bing_(search_engine)> "Bing (search engine)") and [Google](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Search "Google Search"), users worldwide have easy, instant access to a vast and diverse amount of online information. Compared to printed media, books, encyclopedias and traditional libraries, the World Wide Web has enabled the decentralization of information on a large scale.

The Web has enabled individuals and organizations to [publish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publish "Publish") ideas and information to a potentially large [audience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audience "Audience") online at greatly reduced expense and time delay. Publishing a web page, a blog, or building a website involves little initial [cost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost "Cost") and many cost-free services are available. However, publishing and maintaining large, professional websites with attractive, diverse and up-to-date information is still a difficult and expensive proposition. Many individuals and some companies and groups use _web logs_ or blogs, which are largely used as easily being able to update online diaries. Some commercial organizations encourage [staff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Employees "Employees") to communicate advice in their areas of specialization in the hope that visitors will be impressed by the expert knowledge and free information and be attracted to the corporation as a result.

[Advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_advertising "Online advertising") on popular web pages can be lucrative, and [e-commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-commerce "E-commerce"), which is the sale of products and services directly via the Web, continues to grow. Online advertising is a form of [marketing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marketing "Marketing") and advertising which uses the Internet to deliver [promotional](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Promotion_(marketing)> "Promotion (marketing)") marketing messages to consumers. It includes email marketing, [search engine marketing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Search_engine_marketing "Search engine marketing") (SEM), social media marketing, many types of [display advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Display_advertising "Display advertising") (including [web banner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_banner "Web banner") advertising), and [mobile advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_advertising "Mobile advertising"). In 2011, Internet advertising revenues in the United States surpassed those of [cable television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable_television "Cable television") and nearly exceeded those of [broadcast television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast_television "Broadcast television").<sup id="cite_ref-IAB2012_98-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-IAB2012-98"><span>[</span>96<span>]</span></a></sup><sup><span title="Page / location: 19">: 19 </span></sup>  Many common online advertising practices are controversial and increasingly subject to regulation.

When the Web developed in the 1990s, a typical web page was stored in completed form on a web server, formatted in [HTML](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTML "HTML"), ready for transmission to a web browser in response to a request. Over time, the process of creating and serving web pages has become dynamic, creating a flexible design, layout, and content. Websites are often created using [content management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Content_management "Content management") software with, initially, very little content. Contributors to these systems, who may be paid staff, members of an organization or the public, fill underlying databases with content using editing pages designed for that purpose while casual visitors view and read this content in HTML form. There may or may not be editorial, approval and security systems built into the process of taking newly entered content and making it available to the target visitors.

### Communication

[Email](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Email "Email") is an important communications service available via the Internet. The concept of sending electronic text messages between parties, analogous to mailing letters or memos, predates the creation of the Internet.<sup id="cite_ref-99"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-99"><span>[</span>97<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-100"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-100"><span>[</span>98<span>]</span></a></sup> Pictures, documents, and other files are sent as [email attachments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Email_attachment "Email attachment"). Email messages can be [cc-ed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_copy "Carbon copy") to multiple [email addresses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Email_address "Email address"). Numerous [instant messenger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant_messenger "Instant messenger") applications that use the Internet for communication also exist, but with little interoperability.

[Internet telephony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_telephony "Internet telephony") is a common communications service realized with the Internet. The name of the principal internetworking protocol, the Internet Protocol, lends its name to [voice over Internet Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice_over_Internet_Protocol "Voice over Internet Protocol") (VoIP). The idea began in the early 1990s with [walkie-talkie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walkie-talkie "Walkie-talkie")\-like voice applications for personal computers. VoIP systems now dominate many markets and are as easy to use and as convenient as a traditional telephone. The benefit has been substantial cost savings over traditional telephone calls, especially over long distances. [Cable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable_modem "Cable modem"), [ADSL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ADSL "ADSL"), and [mobile data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_data "Mobile data") networks provide [Internet access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_access "Internet access") in customer premises<sup id="cite_ref-EBSCOhost_101-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-EBSCOhost-101"><span>[</span>99<span>]</span></a></sup> and inexpensive VoIP network adapters provide the connection for traditional analog telephone sets. The voice quality of VoIP often exceeds that of traditional calls. Remaining problems for VoIP include the situation that [emergency services numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_services_number "Emergency services number") may not be universally available and that devices rely on a local power supply, while older traditional phones are powered from the [local loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_loop "Local loop"), and typically operate during a power failure.

### File sharing

[File sharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File_sharing "File sharing") is an example of transferring large amounts of data across the Internet. A [computer file](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_file "Computer file") can be emailed to customers, colleagues and friends as an attachment. It can be uploaded to a website or [File Transfer Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File_Transfer_Protocol "File Transfer Protocol") (FTP) server for easy download by others. It can be put into a "shared location" or onto a [file server](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File_server "File server") for instant use by colleagues. The load of bulk downloads to many users can be eased by the use of "[mirror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_mirror "Web mirror")" servers or [peer-to-peer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer-to-peer "Peer-to-peer") networks. In any of these cases, access to the file may be controlled by user [authentication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authentication "Authentication"), the transit of the file over the Internet may be obscured by [encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encryption "Encryption"), and money may change hands for access to the file. The price can be paid by the remote charging of funds from, for example, a credit card whose details are also passed—usually fully encrypted—across the Internet. The origin and authenticity of the file received may be checked by [digital signatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signature "Digital signature") or by [MD5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MD5 "MD5") or other message digests.

These simple features of the Internet, over a worldwide basis, are changing the production, sale, and distribution of anything that can be reduced to a computer file for transmission. This includes all manner of print publications, software products, news, music, film, video, photography, graphics and the other arts. This in turn has caused seismic shifts in each of the existing industries that previously controlled the production and distribution of these products.

### Media distribution

#### Streaming media

[Streaming media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streaming_media "Streaming media") is the real-time delivery of digital media for immediate consumption or enjoyment by end users. Many radio and television broadcasters provide Internet feeds of their live audio and video productions. They may also allow time-shift viewing or listening such as Preview, Classic Clips and Listen Again features. This means that an Internet-connected device, such as a computer or something more specific, can be used to access online media in much the same way as was previously possible only with a television or radio receiver. The range of available types of content is much wider, from specialized technical [webcasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Webcast "Webcast") to on-demand popular multimedia services. [Podcasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podcast "Podcast") is a variation on this theme, where—usually audio—material is downloaded and played back on a computer or shifted to a [portable media player](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portable_media_player "Portable media player") to be listened to on the move. These techniques using simple equipment allow anybody, with little censorship or licensing control, to broadcast audio-visual material worldwide. Digital media streaming increases the demand for network bandwidth. For example, video streams typically need 1 Mbit/s link speed for old-fashioned 480p "standard definition" video, 4.5 Mbit/s for 1080p "Full HD" video.<sup id="cite_ref-102"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-102"><span>[</span>100<span>]</span></a></sup> and up to 40 to 80 Mbps for high-quality [4K video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4K_video "4K video").<sup id="cite_ref-103"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-103"><span>[</span>101<span>]</span></a></sup>

These providers have been joined by a range of pure Internet "broadcasters" who never had on-air licenses, including [Netflix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netflix "Netflix"), [Disney+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disney%2B "Disney+"), Amazon's [Prime Video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_Prime_Video "Amazon Prime Video"), [Mubi](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mubi_(streaming_service)> "Mubi (streaming service)"), [Hulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hulu "Hulu"), and [Apple TV+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_TV%2B "Apple TV+"). These streaming companies now dominate the entertainment industry, eclipsing traditional broadcasters.<sup id="cite_ref-104"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-104"><span>[</span>102<span>]</span></a></sup> Audio streamers such as [Spotify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spotify "Spotify") and [Apple Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Music "Apple Music") also have significant market share in the audio entertainment market.

[Webcams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Webcam "Webcam") are a low-cost extension of this phenomenon. Internet users can watch animals around an African waterhole, ships in the [Panama Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panama_Canal "Panama Canal"), traffic at a local roundabout or monitor their own premises, live and in real time. Video [chat rooms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chat_rooms "Chat rooms") and [video conferencing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_conferencing "Video conferencing") are also popular with many uses being found for personal webcams, with and without two-way sound.

#### Video sharing sites

[Video sharing websites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_sharing_website "Video sharing website") are also a major factor in the entertainment ecosystem. YouTube was founded on 15 February 2005 and is now the leading website for free streaming video with more than two billion users.<sup id="cite_ref-105"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-105"><span>[</span>103<span>]</span></a></sup> It uses a web player by default to stream and show video files.<sup id="cite_ref-106"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-106"><span>[</span>104<span>]</span></a></sup> Registered users may upload an unlimited amount of video and build their own personal profile. [YouTube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YouTube "YouTube") claims that its users watch hundreds of millions, and upload hundreds of thousands of videos daily. Other video sharing websites include [Vimeo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vimeo "Vimeo"), [Instagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instagram "Instagram") and [TikTok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TikTok "TikTok").

A number of advertising-funded ostensible video sharing websites known as "[tube sites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tube_site "Tube site")" were created to host shared [pornographic video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pornographic_video "Pornographic video") content. Due to laws requiring the documentation of the origin of pornography, these websites now largely operate in conjunction with [pornographic movie studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pornographic_movie_studio&action=edit&redlink=1 "Pornographic movie studio (page does not exist)") and their own independent creator networks, acting as de-facto video streaming services. Major players in this field include the market leader [Aylo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aylo "Aylo"), the operator of [PornHub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PornHub "PornHub") and numerous other branded sites, as well as other independent operators such as [xHamster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XHamster "XHamster") and [Xvideos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xvideos "Xvideos"). As of 2023, Internet traffic to pornographic video sites rivalled that of mainstream video streaming and sharing services.<sup id="cite_ref-107"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-107"><span>[</span>105<span>]</span></a></sup> Other non-advertising-funded sites such as [Clips4sale](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Clips4sale&action=edit&redlink=1 "Clips4sale (page does not exist)") operate on a paid content basis.

#### Live streaming

[Live streaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_streaming "Live streaming") services allow the live transmission of video on a one-to-one or one-to-many basis from online content creators to niche audiences. Common content genres include [video gaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_gaming "Video gaming") and [personalized pornography](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Personalized_pornography&action=edit&redlink=1 "Personalized pornography (page does not exist)"). Well-known services include [Twitch](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twitch_(service)> "Twitch (service)") in the first category and [OnlyFans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OnlyFans "OnlyFans") in the second.

The Internet has enabled new forms of social interaction, activities, and social associations. This phenomenon has given rise to the scholarly study of the [sociology of the Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology_of_the_Internet "Sociology of the Internet"). The early Internet left an impact on some [writers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writer "Writer") who used [symbolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbol "Symbol") to write about it, such as describing the Internet as a "means to connect individuals in a vast invisible net over all the [earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth "Earth")."<sup id="cite_ref-108"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-108"><span>[</span>106<span>]</span></a></sup>

Between 2000 and 2009, the number of Internet users globally rose from 390 million to 1.9 billion.<sup id="cite_ref-112"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-112"><span>[</span>110<span>]</span></a></sup> By 2010, 22% of the world's population had access to computers with 1 billion [Google](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google "Google") searches every day, 300 million Internet users reading blogs, and 2 billion videos viewed daily on [YouTube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YouTube "YouTube").<sup id="cite_ref-113"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-113"><span>[</span>111<span>]</span></a></sup> In 2014 the world's Internet users surpassed 3 billion or 44 percent of world population, but two-thirds came from the richest countries, with 78 percent of Europeans using the Internet, followed by 57 percent of the Americas.<sup id="cite_ref-114"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-114"><span>[</span>112<span>]</span></a></sup> However, by 2018, Asia alone accounted for 51% of all Internet users, with 2.2 billion out of the 4.3 billion Internet users in the world. China's Internet users surpassed a major milestone in 2018, when the country's Internet regulatory authority, China Internet Network Information Centre, announced that China had 802 million users.<sup id="cite_ref-115"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-115"><span>[</span>113<span>]</span></a></sup> China was followed by India, with some 700 million users, with the United States third with 275 million users. However, in terms of penetration, in 2022 China had a 70% penetration rate compared to India's 60% and the United States's 90%.<sup id="cite_ref-116"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-116"><span>[</span>114<span>]</span></a></sup> In 2022, 54% of the world's Internet users were based in Asia, 14% in Europe, 7% in North America, 10% in Latin America and the [Caribbean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caribbean "Caribbean"), 11% in Africa, 4% in the Middle East and 1% in Oceania.<sup id="cite_ref-inetstats_117-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-inetstats-117"><span>[</span>115<span>]</span></a></sup> In 2019, Kuwait, Qatar, the Falkland Islands, Bermuda and Iceland had the highest [Internet penetration by the number of users](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_number_of_Internet_users "List of countries by number of Internet users"), with 93% or more of the population with access.<sup id="cite_ref-ITU-IndividualsUsingTheInternet_118-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-ITU-IndividualsUsingTheInternet-118"><span>[</span>116<span>]</span></a></sup> As of 2022, it was estimated that 5.4 billion people use the Internet, more than two-thirds of the world's population.<sup id="cite_ref-119"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-119"><span>[</span>117<span>]</span></a></sup>

The prevalent language for communication via the Internet has always been English. This may be a result of the origin of the Internet, as well as the language's role as a [lingua franca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingua_franca "Lingua franca") and as a [world language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_language "World language"). Early computer systems were limited to the characters in the [American Standard Code for Information Interchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASCII "ASCII") (ASCII), a subset of the [Latin alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_alphabet "Latin alphabet"). After English (27%), the most requested languages on the World Wide Web are Chinese (25%), Spanish (8%), Japanese (5%), Portuguese and German (4% each), Arabic, French and Russian (3% each), and Korean (2%).<sup id="cite_ref-NIUBL-IWS_120-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-NIUBL-IWS-120"><span>[</span>118<span>]</span></a></sup> The Internet's technologies have developed enough in recent years, especially in the use of [Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode "Unicode"), that good facilities are available for development and communication in the world's widely used languages. However, some glitches such as _[mojibake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mojibake "Mojibake")_ (incorrect display of some languages' characters) still remain.

In a US study in 2005, the percentage of men using the Internet was very slightly ahead of the percentage of women, although this difference reversed in those under 30. Men logged on more often, spent more time online, and were more likely to be broadband users, whereas women tended to make more use of opportunities to communicate (such as email). Men were more likely to use the Internet to pay bills, participate in auctions, and for recreation such as downloading music and videos. Men and women were equally likely to use the Internet for shopping and banking.<sup id="cite_ref-121"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-121"><span>[</span>119<span>]</span></a></sup> In 2008, women significantly outnumbered men on most social networking services, such as Facebook and Myspace, although the ratios varied with age.<sup id="cite_ref-122"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-122"><span>[</span>120<span>]</span></a></sup> Women watched more streaming content, whereas men downloaded more.<sup id="cite_ref-123"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-123"><span>[</span>121<span>]</span></a></sup> Men were more likely to blog. Among those who blog, men were more likely to have a professional blog, whereas women were more likely to have a personal blog.<sup id="cite_ref-124"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-124"><span>[</span>122<span>]</span></a></sup>

The Internet allows greater flexibility in working hours and location, especially with the spread of unmetered high-speed connections. The Internet can be accessed almost anywhere by numerous means, including through [mobile Internet devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_Internet_device "Mobile Internet device"). Mobile phones, [datacards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Datacard "Datacard"), [handheld game consoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handheld_game_console "Handheld game console") and [cellular routers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_router "Cellular router") allow users to connect to the Internet [wirelessly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless "Wireless"). Within the limitations imposed by small screens and other limited facilities of such pocket-sized devices, the services of the Internet, including email and the web, may be available. Service providers may restrict the services offered and mobile data charges may be significantly higher than other access methods.

Educational material at all levels from pre-school to post-doctoral is available from websites. Examples range from [CBeebies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBeebies "CBeebies"), through school and high-school revision guides and [virtual universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_university "Virtual university"), to access to top-end scholarly literature through the likes of [Google Scholar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Scholar "Google Scholar"). For [distance education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distance_education "Distance education"), help with [homework](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homework "Homework") and other assignments, self-guided learning, while away spare time or just looking up more detail on an interesting fact, it has never been easier for people to access educational information at any level from anywhere. The Internet in general and the World Wide Web in particular are important enablers of both [formal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education "Education") and [informal education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Informal_education "Informal education"). Further, the Internet allows researchers (especially those from the social and behavioral sciences) to conduct research remotely via virtual laboratories, with profound changes in reach and generalizability of findings as well as in communication between scientists and in the publication of results.<sup id="cite_ref-134"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-134"><span>[</span>132<span>]</span></a></sup>

The low cost and nearly instantaneous sharing of ideas, knowledge, and skills have made [collaborative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collaboration "Collaboration") work dramatically easier, with the help of [collaborative software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collaborative_software "Collaborative software"). Not only can a group cheaply communicate and share ideas but the wide reach of the Internet allows such groups more easily to form. An example of this is the [free software movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_software_movement "Free software movement"), which has produced, among other things, [Linux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linux "Linux"), [Mozilla Firefox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozilla_Firefox "Mozilla Firefox"), and [OpenOffice.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenOffice.org "OpenOffice.org") (later forked into [LibreOffice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LibreOffice "LibreOffice")). Internet chat, whether using an [IRC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IRC "IRC") chat room, an [instant messaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant_messaging "Instant messaging") system, or a social networking service, allows colleagues to stay in touch in a very convenient way while working at their computers during the day. Messages can be exchanged even more quickly and conveniently than via email. These systems may allow files to be exchanged, drawings and images to be shared, or voice and video contact between team members.

[Content management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Content_management "Content management") systems allow collaborating teams to work on shared sets of documents simultaneously without accidentally destroying each other's work. Business and project teams can share calendars as well as documents and other information. Such collaboration occurs in a wide variety of areas including scientific research, software development, conference planning, political activism and creative writing. Social and political collaboration is also becoming more widespread as both Internet access and [computer literacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_literacy "Computer literacy") spread.

The Internet allows computer users to remotely access other computers and information stores easily from any access point. Access may be with [computer security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_security "Computer security"); i.e., authentication and encryption technologies, depending on the requirements. This is encouraging new ways of [remote work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_work "Remote work"), collaboration and information sharing in many industries. An accountant sitting at home can [audit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audit "Audit") the books of a company based in another country, on a server situated in a third country that is remotely maintained by IT specialists in a fourth. These accounts could have been created by home-working bookkeepers, in other remote locations, based on information emailed to them from offices all over the world. Some of these things were possible before the widespread use of the Internet, but the cost of private [leased lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leased_line "Leased line") would have made many of them infeasible in practice. An office worker away from their desk, perhaps on the other side of the world on a business trip or a holiday, can access their emails, access their data using [cloud computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_computing "Cloud computing"), or open a [remote desktop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_Desktop_Protocol "Remote Desktop Protocol") session into their office PC using a secure [virtual private network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_private_network "Virtual private network") (VPN) connection on the Internet. This can give the worker complete access to all of their normal files and data, including email and other applications, while away from the office. It has been referred to among [system administrators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/System_administrator "System administrator") as the Virtual Private Nightmare,<sup id="cite_ref-135"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-135"><span>[</span>133<span>]</span></a></sup> because it extends the secure perimeter of a corporate network into remote locations and its employees' homes. By the late 2010s the Internet had been described as "the main source of scientific information "for the majority of the global North population".<sup id="cite_ref-136"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-136"><span>[</span>134<span>]</span></a></sup><sup><span title="Page / location: 111">: 111 </span></sup>

### Social networking and entertainment

Many people use the World Wide Web to access news, weather and sports reports, to plan and book vacations and to pursue their personal interests. People use [chat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_chat "Online chat"), messaging and email to make and stay in touch with friends worldwide, sometimes in the same way as some previously had [pen pals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pen_pal "Pen pal"). Social networking services such as [Facebook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facebook "Facebook") have created new ways to socialize and interact. Users of these sites are able to add a wide variety of information to pages, pursue common interests, and connect with others. It is also possible to find existing acquaintances, to allow communication among existing groups of people. Sites like [LinkedIn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LinkedIn "LinkedIn") foster commercial and business connections. YouTube and [Flickr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flickr "Flickr") specialize in users' videos and photographs. Social networking services are also widely used by businesses and other organizations to promote their brands, to market to their customers and to encourage posts to "[go viral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viral_marketing "Viral marketing")". "Black hat" social media techniques are also employed by some organizations, such as [spam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spamming "Spamming") accounts and [astroturfing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astroturfing "Astroturfing").

A risk for both individuals' and organizations' writing posts (especially public posts) on social networking services is that especially foolish or controversial posts occasionally lead to an unexpected and possibly large-scale backlash on social media from other Internet users. This is also a risk in relation to controversial _offline_ behavior, if it is widely made known. The nature of this backlash can range widely from counter-arguments and public mockery, through insults and [hate speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hate_speech "Hate speech"), to, in extreme cases, rape and death [threats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_crime#Online_harassment "Computer crime"). The [online disinhibition effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_disinhibition_effect "Online disinhibition effect") describes the tendency of many individuals to behave more stridently or offensively online than they would in person. A significant number of [feminist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feminist "Feminist") women have been the target of various forms of [harassment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harassment "Harassment") in response to posts they have made on social media, and Twitter in particular has been criticized in the past for not doing enough to aid victims of online abuse.<sup id="cite_ref-137"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-137"><span>[</span>135<span>]</span></a></sup>

For organizations, such a backlash can cause overall [brand damage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_relations "Public relations"), especially if reported by the media. However, this is not always the case, as any brand damage in the eyes of people with an opposing opinion to that presented by the organization could sometimes be outweighed by strengthening the brand in the eyes of others. Furthermore, if an organization or individual gives in to demands that others perceive as wrong-headed, that can then provoke a counter-backlash.

Some websites, such as [Reddit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reddit "Reddit"), have rules forbidding the posting of [personal information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_information "Personal information") of individuals (also known as [doxxing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doxxing "Doxxing")), due to concerns about such postings leading to mobs of large numbers of Internet users directing harassment at the specific individuals thereby identified. In particular, the Reddit rule forbidding the posting of personal information is widely understood to imply that all identifying photos and names must be [censored](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censored "Censored") in Facebook [screenshots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screenshots "Screenshots") posted to Reddit. However, the interpretation of this rule in relation to public Twitter posts is less clear, and in any case, like-minded people online have many other ways they can use to direct each other's attention to public social media posts they disagree with.

Children also face dangers online such as [cyberbullying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyberbullying "Cyberbullying") and [approaches by sexual predators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_grooming "Child grooming"), who sometimes pose as children themselves. Children may also encounter material that they may find upsetting, or material that their parents consider to be not age-appropriate. Due to naivety, they may also post personal information about themselves online, which could put them or their families at risk unless warned not to do so. Many parents choose to enable [Internet filtering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Content-control_software "Content-control software") or supervise their children's online activities in an attempt to protect their children from inappropriate material on the Internet. The most popular social networking services, such as Facebook and Twitter, commonly forbid users under the age of 13. However, these policies are typically trivial to circumvent by registering an account with a false birth date, and a significant number of children aged under 13 join such sites anyway. Social networking services for younger children, which claim to provide better levels of protection for children, also exist.<sup id="cite_ref-138"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-138"><span>[</span>136<span>]</span></a></sup>

The Internet has been a major outlet for leisure activity since its inception, with entertaining [social experiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_experiment "Social experiment") such as [MUDs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-user_dungeon "Multi-user dungeon") and [MOOs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOO "MOO") being conducted on university servers, and humor-related [Usenet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usenet "Usenet") groups receiving much traffic.<sup id="cite_ref-StudFiles_139-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-StudFiles-139"><span>[</span>137<span>]</span></a></sup> Many [Internet forums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_forums "Internet forums") have sections devoted to games and funny videos.<sup id="cite_ref-StudFiles_139-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-StudFiles-139"><span>[</span>137<span>]</span></a></sup> The [Internet pornography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_pornography "Internet pornography") and [online gambling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_gambling "Online gambling") industries have taken advantage of the World Wide Web. Although many governments have attempted to restrict both industries' use of the Internet, in general, this has failed to stop their widespread popularity.<sup id="cite_ref-140"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-140"><span>[</span>138<span>]</span></a></sup>

Another area of leisure activity on the Internet is [multiplayer gaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplayer_gaming "Multiplayer gaming").<sup id="cite_ref-141"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-141"><span>[</span>139<span>]</span></a></sup> This form of recreation creates communities, where people of all ages and origins enjoy the fast-paced world of multiplayer games. These range from [MMORPG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MMORPG "MMORPG") to [first-person shooters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-person_shooter "First-person shooter"), from [role-playing video games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Role-playing_video_game "Role-playing video game") to [online gambling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_gambling "Online gambling"). While online gaming has been around since the 1970s, modern modes of online gaming began with subscription services such as [GameSpy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GameSpy_Arcade "GameSpy Arcade") and [MPlayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MPlayer.com "MPlayer.com").<sup id="cite_ref-142"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-142"><span>[</span>140<span>]</span></a></sup> Non-subscribers were limited to certain types of game play or certain games. Many people use the Internet to access and download music, movies and other works for their enjoyment and relaxation. Free and fee-based services exist for all of these activities, using centralized servers and distributed peer-to-peer technologies. Some of these sources exercise more care with respect to the original artists' copyrights than others.

Internet usage has been correlated to users' loneliness.<sup id="cite_ref-143"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-143"><span>[</span>141<span>]</span></a></sup> Lonely people tend to use the Internet as an outlet for their feelings and to share their stories with others, such as in the "[I am lonely will anyone speak to me](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_am_lonely_will_anyone_speak_to_me "I am lonely will anyone speak to me")" thread. A 2017 book claimed that the Internet consolidates most aspects of human endeavor into singular arenas of which all of humanity are potential members and competitors, with fundamentally negative [impacts on mental health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_media_use_and_mental_health "Digital media use and mental health") as a result. While successes in each field of activity are pervasively visible and trumpeted, they are reserved for an extremely thin sliver of the world's most exceptional, leaving everyone else behind. Whereas, before the Internet, expectations of success in any field were supported by reasonable probabilities of achievement at the village, suburb, city or even state level, the same expectations in the Internet world are virtually certain to bring disappointment today: there is always someone else, somewhere on the planet, who can do better and take the now one-and-only top spot.<sup id="cite_ref-144"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-144"><span>[</span>142<span>]</span></a></sup>

[Cybersectarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cybersectarianism "Cybersectarianism") is a new organizational form that involves, "highly dispersed small groups of practitioners that may remain largely anonymous within the larger social context and operate in relative secrecy, while still linked remotely to a larger network of believers who share a set of practices and texts, and often a common devotion to a particular leader. Overseas supporters provide funding and support; domestic practitioners distribute tracts, participate in acts of resistance, and share information on the internal situation with outsiders. Collectively, members and practitioners of such sects construct viable virtual communities of faith, exchanging personal testimonies and engaging in the collective study via email, online chat rooms, and web-based message boards."<sup id="cite_ref-145"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-145"><span>[</span>143<span>]</span></a></sup> In particular, the British government has raised concerns about the prospect of young British Muslims being indoctrinated into Islamic extremism by material on the Internet, being persuaded to join [terrorist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrorist "Terrorist") groups such as the so-called "[Islamic State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_State_of_Iraq_and_the_Levant "Islamic State of Iraq and the Levant")", and then potentially committing acts of terrorism on returning to Britain after fighting in Syria or Iraq.

[Cyberslacking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyberslacking "Cyberslacking") can become a drain on corporate resources; the average UK employee spent 57 minutes a day surfing the Web while at work, according to a 2003 study by Peninsula Business Services.<sup id="cite_ref-146"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-146"><span>[</span>144<span>]</span></a></sup> [Internet addiction disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_addiction_disorder "Internet addiction disorder") is excessive computer use that interferes with daily life. [Nicholas G. Carr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_G._Carr "Nicholas G. Carr") believes that Internet use has other [effects on individuals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_effects_of_Internet_use "Psychological effects of Internet use"), for instance improving skills of scan-reading and [interfering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interference_theory "Interference theory") with the deep thinking that leads to true creativity.<sup id="cite_ref-147"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-147"><span>[</span>145<span>]</span></a></sup>

### Electronic business

[Electronic business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_business "Electronic business") (_e-business_) encompasses business processes spanning the entire [value chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_chain "Value chain"): purchasing, [supply chain management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supply_chain_management "Supply chain management"), [marketing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marketing "Marketing"), [sales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sales "Sales"), [customer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customer "Customer") service, and business relationship. [E-commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-commerce "E-commerce") seeks to add revenue streams using the Internet to build and enhance relationships with clients and partners. According to [International Data Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Data_Corporation "International Data Corporation"), the size of worldwide e-commerce, when global business-to-business and -consumer transactions are combined, equate to $16 trillion for 2013. A report by Oxford Economics added those two together to estimate the total size of the [digital economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_economy "Digital economy") at $20.4 trillion, equivalent to roughly 13.8% of global sales.<sup id="cite_ref-148"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-148"><span>[</span>146<span>]</span></a></sup>

While much has been written of the economic advantages of [Internet-enabled commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_commerce "Electronic commerce"), there is also evidence that some aspects of the Internet such as maps and location-aware services may serve to reinforce [economic inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_inequality "Economic inequality") and the [digital divide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_divide "Digital divide").<sup id="cite_ref-149"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-149"><span>[</span>147<span>]</span></a></sup> Electronic commerce may be responsible for [consolidation](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consolidation_(business)> "Consolidation (business)") and the decline of [mom-and-pop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mom-and-pop "Mom-and-pop"), [brick and mortar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brick_and_mortar "Brick and mortar") businesses resulting in increases in [income inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_inequality "Income inequality").<sup id="cite_ref-150"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-150"><span>[</span>148<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-151"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-151"><span>[</span>149<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-152"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-152"><span>[</span>150<span>]</span></a></sup>

Author [Andrew Keen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Keen "Andrew Keen"), a long-time critic of the social transformations caused by the Internet, has focused on the economic effects of consolidation from Internet businesses. Keen cites a 2013 [Institute for Local Self-Reliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Local_Self-Reliance "Institute for Local Self-Reliance") report saying brick-and-mortar retailers employ 47 people for every $10 million in sales while Amazon employs only 14. Similarly, the 700-employee room rental start-up [Airbnb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbnb "Airbnb") was valued at $10 billion in 2014, about half as much as [Hilton Worldwide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilton_Worldwide "Hilton Worldwide"), which employs 152,000 people. At that time, [Uber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uber "Uber") employed 1,000 full-time employees and was valued at $18.2 billion, about the same valuation as [Avis Rent a Car](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avis_Rent_a_Car "Avis Rent a Car") and [The Hertz Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hertz_Corporation "The Hertz Corporation") combined, which together employed almost 60,000 people.<sup id="cite_ref-153"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-153"><span>[</span>151<span>]</span></a></sup>

### Remote work

[Remote work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_work "Remote work") is facilitated by tools such as [groupware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groupware "Groupware"), [virtual private networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_private_networks "Virtual private networks"), [conference calling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conference_calling "Conference calling"), [videotelephony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videotelephony "Videotelephony"), and VoIP so that work may be performed from any location, most conveniently the worker's home. It can be efficient and useful for companies as it allows workers to communicate over long distances, saving significant amounts of travel time and cost. More workers have adequate bandwidth at home to use these tools to link their home to their corporate [intranet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intranet "Intranet") and internal communication networks.

### Collaborative publishing

[Wikis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiki "Wiki") have also been used in the academic community for sharing and dissemination of information across institutional and international boundaries.<sup id="cite_ref-154"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-154"><span>[</span>152<span>]</span></a></sup> In those settings, they have been found useful for collaboration on [grant writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grant_writing "Grant writing"), [strategic planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_planning "Strategic planning"), departmental documentation, and committee work.<sup id="cite_ref-155"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-155"><span>[</span>153<span>]</span></a></sup> The [United States Patent and Trademark Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Patent_and_Trademark_Office "United States Patent and Trademark Office") uses a wiki to allow the public to collaborate on finding [prior art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prior_art "Prior art") relevant to examination of pending patent applications. [Queens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queens "Queens"), New York has used a wiki to allow citizens to collaborate on the design and planning of a local park.<sup id="cite_ref-Noveck_156-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-Noveck-156"><span>[</span>154<span>]</span></a></sup> The [English Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Wikipedia "English Wikipedia") has the largest user base among wikis on the World Wide Web<sup id="cite_ref-157"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-157"><span>[</span>155<span>]</span></a></sup> and ranks in the top 10 among all sites in terms of traffic.<sup id="cite_ref-158"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-158"><span>[</span>156<span>]</span></a></sup>

### Politics and political revolutions

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/01/Thai-coup-detat-2014-social-media-banner.jpg/250px-Thai-coup-detat-2014-social-media-banner.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Thai-coup-detat-2014-social-media-banner.jpg)

Banner in [Bangkok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangkok "Bangkok") during the [2014 Thai coup d'état](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Thai_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat "2014 Thai coup d'état"), informing the [Thai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thailand "Thailand") public that 'like' or 'share' activities on social media could result in imprisonment (observed 30 June 2014)

The Internet has achieved new relevance as a political tool. The presidential campaign of [Howard Dean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Dean "Howard Dean") in 2004 in the United States was notable for its success in soliciting donation via the Internet. Many political groups use the Internet to achieve a new method of organizing for carrying out their mission, having given rise to [Internet activism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_activism "Internet activism").<sup id="cite_ref-cascading_159-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-cascading-159"><span>[</span>157<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-160"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-160"><span>[</span>158<span>]</span></a></sup> _[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")_ suggested that [social media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_media "Social media") websites, such as Facebook and Twitter, helped people organize the political revolutions in Egypt, by helping activists organize protests, communicate grievances, and disseminate information.<sup id="cite_ref-161"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-161"><span>[</span>159<span>]</span></a></sup>

Many have understood the Internet as an extension of the [Habermasian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%BCrgen_Habermas "Jürgen Habermas") notion of the _[public sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_sphere "Public sphere")_, observing how network communication technologies provide something like a global civic forum. However, incidents of politically motivated [Internet censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship "Internet censorship") have now been recorded in many countries, including western democracies.<sup id="cite_ref-DeibertPalfreyRohozinski2008_162-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-DeibertPalfreyRohozinski2008-162"><span>[</span>160<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-DiamondPlattner2012_163-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-DiamondPlattner2012-163"><span>[</span>161<span>]</span></a></sup>

[E-government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-government "E-government") is the use of [technological communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_and_communications_technology "Information and communications technology") devices, such as the Internet, to provide [public services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_service "Public service") to citizens and other persons in a country or region. E-government offers opportunities for more direct and convenient citizen access to government<sup id="cite_ref-164"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-164"><span>[</span>162<span>]</span></a></sup> and for government provision of services directly to citizens.<sup id="cite_ref-165"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-165"><span>[</span>163<span>]</span></a></sup>

### Philanthropy

The spread of low-cost Internet access in developing countries has opened up new possibilities for [peer-to-peer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_peer-to-peer_processes "Social peer-to-peer processes") charities, which allow individuals to contribute small amounts to charitable projects for other individuals. Websites, such as [DonorsChoose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DonorsChoose "DonorsChoose") and [GlobalGiving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GlobalGiving "GlobalGiving"), allow small-scale donors to direct funds to individual projects of their choice. A popular twist on Internet-based philanthropy is the use of [peer-to-peer lending](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer-to-peer_lending "Peer-to-peer lending") for charitable purposes. [Kiva](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiva_(organization)> "Kiva (organization)") pioneered this concept in 2005, offering the first web-based service to publish individual loan profiles for funding. Kiva raises funds for local intermediary [microfinance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microfinance "Microfinance") organizations that post stories and updates on behalf of the borrowers. Lenders can contribute as little as $25 to loans of their choice and receive their money back as borrowers repay. Kiva falls short of being a pure peer-to-peer charity, in that loans are disbursed before being funded by lenders and borrowers do not communicate with lenders themselves.<sup id="cite_ref-166"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-166"><span>[</span>164<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-167"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-167"><span>[</span>165<span>]</span></a></sup>

## Security

Internet resources, hardware, and software components are the target of criminal or malicious attempts to gain unauthorized control to cause interruptions, commit fraud, engage in blackmail or access private information.<sup id="cite_ref-168"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-168"><span>[</span>166<span>]</span></a></sup>

### Malware

[Malware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malware "Malware") is malicious software used and distributed via the Internet. It includes [computer viruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_virus "Computer virus") which are copied with the help of humans, [computer worms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_worm "Computer worm") which copy themselves automatically, software for [denial of service attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denial_of_service_attack "Denial of service attack"), [ransomware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ransomware "Ransomware"), [botnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botnet "Botnet"), and [spyware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spyware "Spyware") that reports on the activity and typing of users. Usually, these activities constitute [cybercrime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cybercrime "Cybercrime"). Defense theorists have also speculated about the possibilities of [hackers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hackers "Hackers") using [cyber warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyber_warfare "Cyber warfare") using similar methods on a large scale.<sup id="cite_ref-169"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-169"><span>[</span>167<span>]</span></a></sup>

Malware poses serious problems to individuals and businesses on the Internet.<sup id="cite_ref-170"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-170"><span>[</span>168<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-171"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-171"><span>[</span>169<span>]</span></a></sup> According to [Symantec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NortonLifeLock "NortonLifeLock")'s 2018 Internet Security Threat Report (ISTR), malware variants number has increased to 669,947,865 in 2017, which is twice as many malware variants as in 2016.<sup id="cite_ref-172"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-172"><span>[</span>170<span>]</span></a></sup> [Cybercrime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cybercrime "Cybercrime"), which includes malware attacks as well as other crimes committed by computer, was predicted to cost the world economy US$6 trillion in 2021, and is increasing at a rate of 15% per year.<sup id="cite_ref-Morgan_173-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-Morgan-173"><span>[</span>171<span>]</span></a></sup> Since 2021, malware has been designed to target computer systems that run critical infrastructure such as the [electricity distribution network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity_infrastructure "Electricity infrastructure").<sup id="cite_ref-174"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-174"><span>[</span>172<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-175"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-175"><span>[</span>173<span>]</span></a></sup> Malware can be designed to evade antivirus software detection algorithms.<sup id="cite_ref-176"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-176"><span>[</span>174<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-177"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-177"><span>[</span>175<span>]</span></a></sup>

### Surveillance

The vast majority of computer surveillance involves the monitoring of [data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_mining "Data mining") and [traffic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_analysis "Traffic analysis") on the Internet.<sup id="cite_ref-sciam-internet_178-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-sciam-internet-178"><span>[</span>176<span>]</span></a></sup> In the United States for example, under the [Communications Assistance For Law Enforcement Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communications_Assistance_For_Law_Enforcement_Act "Communications Assistance For Law Enforcement Act"), all phone calls and broadband Internet traffic (emails, web traffic, instant messaging, etc.) are required to be available for unimpeded real-time monitoring by Federal law enforcement agencies.<sup id="cite_ref-eff-calea-archive_179-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-eff-calea-archive-179"><span>[</span>177<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-eff-calea-summary_180-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-eff-calea-summary-180"><span>[</span>178<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-eff-calea-faq_181-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-eff-calea-faq-181"><span>[</span>179<span>]</span></a></sup> [Packet capture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Packet_capture "Packet capture") is the monitoring of data traffic on a [computer network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_network "Computer network"). Computers communicate over the Internet by breaking up messages (emails, images, videos, web pages, files, etc.) into small chunks called "packets", which are routed through a network of computers, until they reach their destination, where they are assembled back into a complete "message" again. [Packet Capture Appliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Packet_Capture_Appliance "Packet Capture Appliance") intercepts these packets as they are traveling through the network, in order to examine their contents using other programs. A packet capture is an information _gathering_ tool, but not an _analysis_ tool. That is it gathers "messages" but it does not analyze them and figure out what they mean. Other programs are needed to perform [traffic analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_analysis "Traffic analysis") and sift through intercepted data looking for important/useful information. Under the [Communications Assistance For Law Enforcement Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communications_Assistance_For_Law_Enforcement_Act "Communications Assistance For Law Enforcement Act") all U.S. telecommunications providers are required to install packet sniffing technology to allow Federal law enforcement and intelligence agencies to intercept all of their customers' [broadband Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadband_Internet "Broadband Internet") and VoIP traffic.<sup id="cite_ref-182"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-182"><span>[</span>180<span>]</span></a></sup>

The large amount of data gathered from packet capture requires surveillance software that filters and reports relevant information, such as the use of certain words or phrases, the access to certain types of web sites, or communicating via email or chat with certain parties.<sup id="cite_ref-usatoday-chatroom_183-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-usatoday-chatroom-183"><span>[</span>181<span>]</span></a></sup> Agencies, such as the [Information Awareness Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_Awareness_Office "Information Awareness Office"), [NSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NSA "NSA"), [GCHQ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GCHQ "GCHQ") and the [FBI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI "FBI"), spend billions of dollars per year to develop, purchase, implement, and operate systems for interception and analysis of data.<sup id="cite_ref-zdnet-fbi_184-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-zdnet-fbi-184"><span>[</span>182<span>]</span></a></sup> Similar systems are operated by [Iranian secret police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Intelligence_and_National_Security_of_Iran "Ministry of Intelligence and National Security of Iran") to identify and suppress dissidents. The required hardware and software were allegedly installed by German [Siemens AG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens_AG "Siemens AG") and Finnish [Nokia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nokia "Nokia").<sup id="cite_ref-185"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-185"><span>[</span>183<span>]</span></a></sup>

Some governments, such as those of [Burma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burma "Burma"), [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran "Iran"), [North Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship_in_North_Korea "Censorship in North Korea"), [Mainland China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship_in_China "Censorship in China"), [Saudi Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_Arabia "Saudi Arabia") and the [United Arab Emirates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Arab_Emirates "United Arab Emirates"), restrict access to content on the Internet within their territories, especially to political and religious content, with domain name and keyword filters.<sup id="cite_ref-191"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-191"><span>[</span>188<span>]</span></a></sup>

In Norway, Denmark, Finland, and Sweden, major Internet service providers have voluntarily agreed to restrict access to sites listed by authorities. While this list of forbidden resources is supposed to contain only known child pornography sites, the content of the list is secret.<sup id="cite_ref-The_Register_192-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-The_Register-192"><span>[</span>189<span>]</span></a></sup> Many countries, including the United States, have enacted laws against the possession or distribution of certain material, such as [child pornography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_pornography "Child pornography"), via the Internet but do not mandate filter software. Many free or commercially available software programs, called [content-control software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Content-control_software "Content-control software") are available to users to block offensive websites on individual computers or networks in order to limit access by children to pornographic material or depiction of violence.

## Performance

As the Internet is a heterogeneous network, its physical characteristics, including, for example the [data transfer rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit_rate "Bit rate") of connections, vary widely. It exhibits [emergent phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergence#World_Wide_Web_and_the_Internet "Emergence") that depend on its large-scale organization.<sup id="cite_ref-193"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-193"><span>[</span>190<span>]</span></a></sup>

### Traffic volume

The volume of [Internet traffic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_traffic "Internet traffic") is difficult to measure because no single point of measurement exists in the multi-tiered, non-hierarchical topology. Traffic data may be estimated from the aggregate volume through the peering points of the [Tier 1 network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tier_1_network "Tier 1 network") providers, but traffic that stays local in large provider networks may not be accounted for.

### Outages

An [Internet blackout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_blackout "Internet blackout") or outage can be caused by local signaling interruptions. Disruptions of [submarine communications cables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_communications_cable "Submarine communications cable") may cause blackouts or slowdowns to large areas, such as in the [2008 submarine cable disruption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_submarine_cable_disruption "2008 submarine cable disruption"). Less-developed countries are more vulnerable due to the small number of high-capacity links. Land cables are also vulnerable, as in 2011 when a woman digging for scrap metal severed most connectivity for the nation of Armenia.<sup id="cite_ref-194"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-194"><span>[</span>191<span>]</span></a></sup> Internet blackouts affecting almost entire countries can be achieved by governments as a form of [Internet censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship "Internet censorship"), as in the blockage of the [Internet in Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_in_Egypt "Internet in Egypt"), whereby approximately 93%<sup id="cite_ref-renesys1_195-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-renesys1-195"><span>[</span>192<span>]</span></a></sup> of networks were without access in 2011 in an attempt to stop mobilization for [anti-government protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_Revolution_of_2011 "Egyptian Revolution of 2011").<sup id="cite_ref-196"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-196"><span>[</span>193<span>]</span></a></sup>

### Energy use

Estimates of the Internet's [electricity usage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity_usage "Electricity usage") have been the subject of controversy, according to a 2014 peer-reviewed research paper that found claims differing by a factor of 20,000 published in the literature during the preceding decade, ranging from 0.0064 [kilowatt hours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilowatt_hour "Kilowatt hour") per gigabyte transferred (kWh/GB) to 136 kWh/GB.<sup id="cite_ref-Environmental_Impact_Assessment_Review_197-0"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-Environmental_Impact_Assessment_Review-197"><span>[</span>194<span>]</span></a></sup> The researchers attributed these discrepancies mainly to the year of reference (i.e. whether efficiency gains over time had been taken into account) and to whether "end devices such as [personal computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_computer "Personal computer") and servers are included" in the analysis.<sup id="cite_ref-Environmental_Impact_Assessment_Review_197-1"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-Environmental_Impact_Assessment_Review-197"><span>[</span>194<span>]</span></a></sup>

In 2011, academic researchers estimated the overall [energy used](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_use "Energy use") by the Internet to be between 170 and 307 [GW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gigawatt "Gigawatt"), less than two percent of the energy used by humanity. This estimate included the energy needed to build, operate, and periodically replace the estimated 750 million [laptops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laptop "Laptop"), a billion [smart phones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smart_phone "Smart phone") and 100 million servers worldwide as well as the energy that routers, [cell towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_tower "Cell tower"), [optical switches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_switch "Optical switch"), [Wi-Fi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wi-Fi "Wi-Fi") transmitters and [cloud storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_storage "Cloud storage") devices use when transmitting [Internet traffic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_traffic "Internet traffic").<sup id="cite_ref-198"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-198"><span>[</span>195<span>]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-199"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-199"><span>[</span>196<span>]</span></a></sup> According to a non-peer-reviewed study published in 2018 by [The Shift Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shift_Project "The Shift Project") (a French think tank funded by corporate sponsors), nearly 4% of global [CO<sub>2</sub> emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide_emissions "Carbon dioxide emissions") could be attributed to global [data transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_transfer "Data transfer") and the necessary infrastructure.<sup id="cite_ref-200"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-200"><span>[</span>197<span>]</span></a></sup> The study also said that [online video streaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_video "Internet video") alone accounted for 60% of this data transfer and therefore contributed to over 300 million tons of CO<sub>2</sub> emission per year, and argued for new "digital sobriety" regulations restricting the use and size of video files.<sup id="cite_ref-201"><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet#cite_note-201"><span>[</span>198<span>]</span></a></sup>
